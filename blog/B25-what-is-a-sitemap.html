What is a Sitemap? Why Your Website Needs One (2026 Guide)

By RankFrame Team · Updated May 2026 · 14 min read

A sitemap is a file that lists every important page on your website so search engines like Google and Bing can find, crawl, and index them. The most common format is XML, served at a URL like yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml. Without a sitemap, search engines have to discover your pages by following links, which is slower and can leave new or deeply nested pages invisible.

What is a sitemap, exactly?

A sitemap is a structured file that tells search engines which URLs on your site you want them to know about. Think of it as a table of contents for your website, written in a format that crawlers can read in seconds. Instead of relying purely on internal links to discover pages, Google can pull your sitemap, see every URL you care about, and decide which ones to crawl and index.

The term "sitemap" usually refers to an XML file because that is the format Google, Bing, and other search engines officially support. There are also HTML sitemaps, which are user-facing pages, and specialized sitemaps for video, images, and news content. We will cover all of them, but the XML sitemap is the one almost every site needs.

The protocol behind XML sitemaps is open and standardized. It is called the Sitemaps protocol, and it has been jointly supported by Google, Bing, and Yahoo since 2006. That means a single sitemap file works across every major search engine without modification.

Why sitemaps matter for SEO

Google's documentation is clear on this point: a sitemap will not directly boost your rankings, but it will help your pages get discovered and indexed faster. And without indexing, ranking is impossible. So while a sitemap is not a ranking factor, it is a prerequisite for everything else SEO does.

Sitemaps are especially important if

  • Your site is brand new. New domains have no backlinks pointing in, so Googlebot has no natural way to find your pages. A submitted sitemap is often the first signal Google receives that your site exists.
  • Your site is large. Once you have hundreds or thousands of URLs, internal linking alone struggles to expose every page. Crawl budget becomes a real constraint, and a sitemap helps prioritize what gets fetched.
  • Your pages are not well linked internally. Orphan pages (pages with no internal links pointing to them) are invisible to crawlers. A sitemap is sometimes the only way Google learns about them.
  • You have rich media. Image and video sitemaps help Google understand and surface multimedia content in image search and video results.
  • Your site updates frequently. News sites, blogs, and ecommerce stores benefit from fresh sitemaps that signal when content was last modified.
Google's official stance "If your site's pages are properly linked, Google can usually discover most of your site. Even so, a sitemap can improve the crawling of larger or more complex sites, or more specialized files." Sitemaps remain a recommended best practice in 2026.

XML sitemap vs HTML sitemap

The two formats serve different audiences. An XML sitemap exists for search engines. An HTML sitemap exists for humans. Most sites need an XML sitemap, and many sites also publish an HTML sitemap as a backup navigation page, though it is no longer as common as it used to be.

Feature XML sitemap HTML sitemap
Audience Search engine crawlers Human visitors
Format Structured XML file Standard web page with links
Typical URL /sitemap.xml /sitemap or /site-map
Includes metadata Yes (lastmod, priority, etc.) No, just links and labels
Submitted to Google Yes, via Search Console No
Required for SEO Strongly recommended Optional

If you are deciding which to build first, build the XML sitemap. It is the one search engines actually use. An HTML sitemap is nice for accessibility and for users on very large sites, but it has minimal direct SEO impact compared to an XML file.

Anatomy of an XML sitemap

An XML sitemap follows a specific structure defined by the Sitemaps protocol. Here is what a basic sitemap looks like:

<?xml version="1.0" encoding="UTF-8"?> <urlset xmlns="http://www.sitemaps.org/schemas/sitemap/0.9"> <url> <loc>https://example.com/</loc> <lastmod>2026-04-28</lastmod> <changefreq>weekly</changefreq> <priority>1.0</priority> </url> <url> <loc>https://example.com/about</loc> <lastmod>2026-03-12</lastmod> <changefreq>monthly</changefreq> <priority>0.6</priority> </url> <url> <loc>https://example.com/blog/what-is-a-sitemap</loc> <lastmod>2026-05-04</lastmod> <changefreq>monthly</changefreq> <priority>0.8</priority> </url> </urlset>

What each tag means

  • <urlset>: The wrapper element. The xmlns attribute declares the sitemap protocol version.
  • <url>: One block per page on your site.
  • <loc>: The full, absolute URL of the page. This is the only required tag inside <url>. It must use the canonical version (the version with or without www, http vs https, trailing slash or not, that matches what you actually serve).
  • <lastmod>: The date the page was last meaningfully updated, in W3C Datetime format (YYYY-MM-DD). Google uses this signal to decide whether to re-crawl a page.
  • <changefreq>: How often the page changes (always, hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, yearly, never). Google has said this tag is largely ignored in 2026.
  • <priority>: A relative priority from 0.0 to 1.0. Also largely ignored by Google. Bing may still use it.
Heads up: lastmod actually matters Google's John Mueller has confirmed that <lastmod> is one of the few sitemap tags that still influences crawling. But you have to be honest with it. If you set lastmod to today on every URL during every regeneration, Google will eventually start ignoring the value across your entire sitemap.

Sitemap limits to remember

  • Maximum 50,000 URLs per sitemap file.
  • Maximum 50 MB uncompressed per sitemap file.
  • You can gzip the file (sitemap.xml.gz) to save bandwidth, but the uncompressed limit still applies.
  • If your site exceeds either limit, split into multiple sitemaps and use a sitemap index file.

Sitemap index files for huge sites

When a site has more than 50,000 URLs, you cannot put them all in one file. Instead, you create multiple sitemap files (for example sitemap-posts.xml, sitemap-products.xml, sitemap-categories.xml) and then create one sitemap index file that points to all of them.

<?xml version="1.0" encoding="UTF-8"?> <sitemapindex xmlns="http://www.sitemaps.org/schemas/sitemap/0.9"> <sitemap> <loc>https://example.com/sitemap-posts.xml</loc> <lastmod>2026-05-04</lastmod> </sitemap> <sitemap> <loc>https://example.com/sitemap-products.xml</loc> <lastmod>2026-05-03</lastmod> </sitemap> <sitemap> <loc>https://example.com/sitemap-categories.xml</loc> <lastmod>2026-04-30</lastmod> </sitemap> </sitemapindex>

The index file itself is what you submit to Google Search Console. Google will then fetch each child sitemap automatically. This pattern is what large CMS platforms (WordPress with Yoast, Shopify, Webflow) use by default, which is why you often see /sitemap_index.xml as the entry URL on those platforms.

What to include and what to exclude

Your sitemap should be a curated list of the canonical, indexable pages you actually want ranked. It is not a dumping ground for every URL on your domain.

Include

  • Canonical URLs only (the version you want indexed)
  • Pages that return a 200 OK status
  • Pages that you want users to land on from search
  • Important blog posts, product pages, category pages, and landing pages
  • Static informational pages (About, Contact, Pricing)

Exclude

  • Noindex pages. Including a page in your sitemap while it has a noindex meta tag sends conflicting signals. Google has explicitly said this is a mistake.
  • Redirected URLs (301/302). Only include the final destination URL.
  • 404 or 410 pages. Broken URLs in your sitemap waste crawl budget and damage your sitemap's "trust" signal.
  • Non-canonical duplicates. If two URLs serve similar content, only the canonical version belongs in the sitemap.
  • URLs blocked by robots.txt. Google cannot crawl them anyway.
  • Login pages, admin pages, cart pages, internal search results. These add no SEO value.
  • Tag archive and pagination pages (in most cases). They tend to be thin or duplicate content.
The simple rule If you would not be happy seeing a URL in Google's search results, it should not be in your sitemap.

How to find your existing sitemap

Before creating a new sitemap, check whether you already have one. Most modern site builders and CMS platforms generate a sitemap automatically. Here is how to find yours in under a minute.

1. Try the standard locations

In your browser, visit these URLs in order and stop at the first one that loads an XML file:

  • yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml
  • yourdomain.com/sitemap_index.xml
  • yourdomain.com/sitemap1.xml
  • yourdomain.com/sitemap-index.xml

2. Check robots.txt

Visit yourdomain.com/robots.txt. Most sites declare their sitemap at the bottom of this file with a line like:

Sitemap: https://yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml

If you see one or more Sitemap directives, those are the canonical URLs to use.

3. Check Search Console

If your site is verified in Google Search Console, go to Sitemaps in the left sidebar. Any sitemap that has been previously submitted will be listed there with its status and last fetch date.

How to submit your sitemap to Google Search Console

Submitting your sitemap to Google takes about 60 seconds and is one of the most impactful one-time SEO tasks for a new site.

1

Verify your site in Google Search Console

If you haven't already, sign in at search.google.com/search-console with the Google account you want to use. Add your domain as a property and complete verification (DNS TXT record is the most reliable method). You cannot submit a sitemap until your property is verified.

2

Open the Sitemaps report

From the left sidebar, click Indexing > Sitemaps. You will see a field labeled "Add a new sitemap" and a list of any previously submitted sitemaps below it.

Screenshot suggestion: GSC Sitemaps page with "Add a new sitemap" field highlighted.
3

Enter your sitemap path

Type the relative path to your sitemap into the input. For most sites this is simply sitemap.xml. If you use a sitemap index, enter sitemap_index.xml. Do not include the domain, GSC adds it automatically.

4

Click Submit and verify status

Click the blue Submit button. Within a few minutes (sometimes a few hours), the status column will update. You want to see Success with a count of "Discovered URLs" matching what you expect. If you see "Couldn't fetch" or "Has errors," click the row to see specifics.

How to submit your sitemap to Bing Webmaster Tools

Bing powers about 8% of global search and feeds results to other engines like DuckDuckGo and Yahoo. Submitting your sitemap to Bing is worth the five extra minutes.

  1. Go to bing.com/webmasters and sign in with a Microsoft account.
  2. Add your site. The fastest method is Import from Google Search Console, which automatically imports your verified properties and sitemaps in one click.
  3. If importing manually, add your domain, complete verification (DNS TXT, HTML file, or meta tag), and then go to the Sitemaps section under Configuration.
  4. Paste your full sitemap URL (this time you do need the full URL including https://) and click Submit.
  5. Bing typically processes sitemaps within 24 hours.
Pro tip: IndexNow Bing also supports IndexNow, an open protocol that lets you ping search engines instantly when you publish or update a page. If you publish frequently, integrating IndexNow alongside your sitemap can dramatically reduce indexing lag in Bing.

Common sitemap mistakes to avoid

Most sitemap problems fall into a handful of recurring patterns. Avoiding these will put you ahead of the majority of small-to-mid-sized sites.

1. Including broken or redirected URLs

Every URL in your sitemap should return a 200 status and be the final destination. URLs that 301 redirect, 404, or are blocked by robots.txt should be removed. Audit your sitemap with a crawler like Screaming Frog, Sitebulb, or RankFrame's site audit periodically.

2. Including noindex pages

This is one of the most common errors. Listing a URL in your sitemap tells Google "please index this," while a noindex tag on the page tells Google "do not index this." Google will respect the noindex but will treat the conflict as a quality issue.

3. Not regenerating after publishing

If you publish a new page but your sitemap still reflects yesterday's state, that page will be discovered later than necessary. Make sure your platform regenerates the sitemap on every publish (most modern CMS systems do this automatically).

4. Including non-canonical URLs

If example.com and www.example.com both resolve, only one should appear in the sitemap (whichever you have set as your canonical). Same for trailing slashes and tracking parameters.

5. Forgetting to submit it

Having a sitemap is not enough. Google will eventually find it through your robots.txt, but submitting it explicitly via Search Console gets you reporting on indexing status, which is invaluable for diagnosing problems.

6. Putting fake lastmod dates on every URL

Some plugins set every URL's lastmod to today on every regeneration, even when the page has not actually changed. Google has openly said this trains them to ignore your lastmod values entirely. Only update lastmod when the page content has meaningfully changed.

7. Submitting an empty or tiny sitemap

If your sitemap lists 5 URLs but your site has 200 pages, something is wrong with the generator. Check whether pages are being excluded by accident (often this happens when "noindex" is mass-applied to a content type).
