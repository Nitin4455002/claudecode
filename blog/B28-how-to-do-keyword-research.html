How to Do Keyword Research in 2026: Complete Step-by-Step Guide

By RankFrame Team · Updated May 2026 · 17 min read

Keyword research is the process of finding the search terms your audience types into Google, then choosing which of those terms to target with your content. It is the foundation of SEO because every page you publish, every meta title you write, and every backlink you build is wasted if no one is searching for the topic. This guide walks through the full 7-step process from a blank page to a finished keyword map.

What is keyword research?

Keyword research is the practice of discovering the actual words and phrases people type into search engines, evaluating their commercial and traffic potential, and prioritizing the ones your site can realistically rank for. The output of keyword research is not a long messy list. It is a clean, prioritized map that tells you exactly which keyword each page on your site should target.

Most beginners assume they already know what their audience searches for. They are almost always wrong. The vocabulary you use internally, in your own marketing, and on your homepage rarely matches the vocabulary your customers use when they need help. A SaaS company might call their product "team collaboration software" while their customers search for "tool to share files with my team." Keyword research surfaces those gaps and forces you to write in the language your audience actually uses.

Why keyword research matters

Three reasons keyword research is non-negotiable in 2026:

  • It prevents wasted effort. Writing a 3,000 word article on a topic with zero monthly searches will earn you zero organic traffic. Keyword research kills bad content ideas before you spend time on them.
  • It reveals demand and intent. Search volume tells you how many people care about a topic. Search intent tells you what they want when they search. Together they tell you what to build.
  • It maps your competitive landscape. Difficulty scores and SERP analysis show you which keywords are realistic for a new site to rank for and which require domain authority you do not yet have.

Sites that publish without keyword research often produce great writing that nobody finds. Sites that do keyword research first publish less, but every piece is aligned with real demand and earns compounding traffic over time.

The 4 metrics that define a keyword

Every keyword research tool shows roughly the same set of metrics. Understanding what each one means makes the rest of the process click into place.

1. Search volume

The estimated number of times the keyword is searched on Google each month, usually averaged over the prior 12 months. Volumes are estimates, not exact counts. A keyword listed as "1,000 searches per month" might actually receive 600 or 1,500 in any given month. Tools draw from clickstream data, Google Keyword Planner ranges, and proprietary models, so volumes vary across tools.

2. Keyword difficulty (KD)

A 0 to 100 score predicting how hard it is to rank in the top 10 organic results. Most tools calculate KD primarily from the backlink profiles of currently ranking pages. Lower KD means easier to rank. As a rough guide:

  • 0 to 20: easy, often rankable for new sites with solid content
  • 21 to 40: moderate, achievable with focused on-page SEO
  • 41 to 60: hard, requires strong content and some link building
  • 61 to 100: very hard, dominated by high-authority sites

3. Cost per click (CPC)

The average price advertisers pay per click in Google Ads for the keyword. CPC is a strong commercial signal even if you never plan to run ads. High CPC means advertisers are willing to pay for the click, which means the keyword converts to revenue. A keyword with low search volume but a $30 CPC may be more valuable than a keyword with 10x the volume and a $0.20 CPC.

4. Competition

In Google Keyword Planner this is shown as Low, Medium, or High and refers to paid competition (how many advertisers are bidding). It is not the same as organic difficulty. Useful as a secondary signal but do not confuse it with KD.

The 7-step keyword research process

This is the workflow that experienced SEOs use, simplified into seven concrete steps. Run it once for your site and you will have a keyword map you can build content against for the next 6 to 12 months.

1

Brainstorm seed keywords

Open a blank document and list 5 to 15 broad topics that describe what your business does, what your audience cares about, and what problems you solve. These are your seed keywords. They are usually 1 to 3 words and represent entire categories.

For a Framer SEO plugin like RankFrame, seed keywords might be: framer seo, seo plugin, schema markup, keyword tracking, core web vitals, open graph tags, image seo. For a yoga studio, seeds might be: yoga classes, vinyasa, yoga for beginners, meditation, prenatal yoga.

Pull seeds from three sources: your own product or service categories, your sales team's most-asked questions, and the navigation labels of your top 3 competitors. Do not worry about volume or difficulty yet. You are just collecting starting points.

2

Use keyword tools to expand

Feed each seed keyword into a keyword research tool to generate hundreds of related queries. The tool surfaces variations, questions, modifiers, and long-tail forms you would never think of yourself.

Free tools to start with:

  • Google Keyword Planner (inside Google Ads, free with any Ads account) for raw volume and CPC
  • Google Trends for seasonality and breakout terms
  • Google Search Console for keywords your site already gets impressions for
  • AnswerThePublic for question-based long tails
  • Google autocomplete and "People also ask" directly in the SERP

Paid tools that go deeper:

  • Ahrefs for the most accurate backlink-based difficulty scores
  • Semrush for the largest keyword database and best competitor analysis
  • Ubersuggest for a budget-friendly all-in-one
  • RankFrame for keyword research that lives directly inside the Framer editor

Export every keyword the tools suggest into a single spreadsheet. You will likely have 500 to 5,000 rows by the end of this step. Do not filter yet.

3

Analyze search volume, difficulty, CPC, and competition

Now filter the spreadsheet. The exact thresholds depend on your domain authority and patience, but a typical first pass for a new site looks like this:

  • Search volume: at least 50 per month, with most in the 100 to 2,000 range
  • Keyword difficulty: under 30 for early wins, under 50 once you have built some authority
  • CPC: any positive value (signals commercial demand), with bonus weight to keywords above $1

Drop keywords that are clearly off-topic, branded for competitors, or duplicates with trivial wording differences. After this step you should have 100 to 500 viable keywords.

4

Identify search intent

For each remaining keyword, classify the intent. The four standard categories are covered in detail below but the fast version is:

  • Informational: "what is", "how to", "why does"
  • Navigational: "framer login", "rankframe pricing"
  • Commercial: "best", "vs", "review", "alternatives"
  • Transactional: "buy", "download", "free trial", "near me"

The fastest way to verify intent is to actually search the keyword in an incognito Google window and look at the top 10 results. If they are all blog posts, it is informational. If they are all product or category pages, it is transactional. Match the dominant format. Trying to rank a sales page for an informational query almost never works.

5

Group keywords into topic clusters

Bundle keywords that share the same intent and would be satisfied by the same page. For example, all of these belong in one cluster:

  • how to do keyword research
  • keyword research guide
  • keyword research for seo
  • seo keyword research process
  • steps for keyword research

They are all asking the same thing in different words, so they should map to one comprehensive article rather than five thin ones. Two keywords belong in the same cluster if their top 10 SERPs share at least 4 to 5 of the same URLs. This is called SERP overlap and it is the most reliable clustering signal.

6

Map keywords to pages

Build a simple table with one row per cluster. For each row, record:

  • Primary keyword (the highest-volume term in the cluster)
  • Secondary keywords (5 to 20 supporting terms)
  • Search intent (one of the four)
  • Target URL (existing or to be created)
  • Page type (blog post, landing page, product page, comparison)
  • Priority (high, medium, low based on volume, difficulty, and business value)

This map becomes your editorial calendar. Each row is a brief for one piece of content. Do not assign two clusters to the same URL or you will trigger keyword cannibalization, where Google cannot decide which of your pages to rank.

7

Track rankings over time

Once content is live, add the primary and important secondary keywords from each cluster to a rank tracker. Review weekly. You are looking for three things: which keywords are climbing (double down with internal links), which are stuck on page two (refresh the content), and which are dropping (investigate competitors and recent algorithm changes).

Without rank tracking, keyword research is a one-time exercise. With it, keyword research becomes a continuous loop of measure, learn, and refine.

Long-tail vs short-tail keywords

Keywords are often described as short-tail (1 to 2 words, very high volume, very competitive) or long-tail (3 or more words, lower volume each, much easier to rank, often higher intent).

Type Example Monthly volume Difficulty Conversion rate
Short-tail (head) shoes 5,000,000+ 100 Very low
Mid-tail running shoes 500,000 85 Low
Long-tail best running shoes for flat feet 4,000 35 Moderate
Very long-tail best running shoes for flat feet under 100 dollars 120 12 High

New sites should focus 80 percent of their effort on long-tail and very long-tail keywords. The volume per keyword is small, but the cumulative traffic from hundreds of long-tails almost always exceeds what you would have earned chasing one impossible head term. Long-tails also convert better because the searcher's intent is clearer.

The 4 types of search intent

Matching content format to search intent is the highest-leverage decision in modern SEO. Get it wrong and your page will not rank no matter how good the writing.

Informational intent

The searcher wants to learn something. They are not ready to buy. Examples:

  • what is google search console
  • how to do keyword research
  • why is my site not indexed

Best content format: blog posts, guides, tutorials, definitions, FAQ pages.

Navigational intent

The searcher wants a specific website or page. Examples:

  • rankframe login
  • framer marketplace
  • google search console

Best content format: the actual destination page (login, homepage, dashboard). Do not try to outrank a brand for their own name.

Commercial intent

The searcher is researching before a purchase. Examples:

  • best framer seo plugin
  • ahrefs vs semrush
  • rankframe review

Best content format: comparison posts, listicles, reviews, alternative pages.

Transactional intent

The searcher is ready to act. Examples:

  • buy framer seo plugin
  • rankframe pricing
  • install rankframe

Best content format: product pages, pricing pages, signup pages, location pages.

The intent test Before you write, search the keyword and look at the top 10 results. If 8 of them are blog posts, the intent is informational. If 8 are product pages, it is transactional. Build the format Google is already rewarding.

Common keyword research mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing volume only. A keyword with 50,000 monthly searches and KD 85 is worse for a new site than one with 300 searches and KD 15.
  • Ignoring search intent. Writing a sales page for an informational query, or a tutorial for a transactional query.
  • Targeting the same primary keyword on multiple pages. This causes cannibalization. Google splits authority between the two URLs and ranks neither.
  • Trusting one tool's volume blindly. Cross-check between two tools to spot outliers.
  • Skipping the SERP check. Tools cannot tell you the dominant content format. The actual SERP can.
  • Treating it as a one-time task. Search behavior shifts. Refresh your map at least annually.

Frequently asked questions

Keyword research is the process of finding and analyzing the search terms that real users type into search engines, then deciding which of those terms to target with your content. It tells you what topics to write about, how competitive each topic is, and what people actually want when they search for it. Every successful SEO strategy starts with keyword research.
The best free keyword research tools in 2026 are Google Keyword Planner (requires a Google Ads account), Google Trends, Google Search Console (for keywords your site already ranks for), AnswerThePublic (limited free searches per day), and the free tiers of Ubersuggest and Keyword Surfer. Free tools cover most of what beginners need.
Keyword difficulty (KD) is a score, usually from 0 to 100, that estimates how hard it is to rank in the top 10 of Google for a given keyword. It is calculated by analyzing the strength of pages currently ranking, especially their backlink profiles. Lower scores mean easier to rank. Different tools calculate KD differently, so scores from Ahrefs, Semrush, and Moz are not directly comparable.
Search intent is the goal a user has in mind when they type a query. The four main types are informational (looking for information), navigational (looking for a specific site), commercial (researching before a purchase), and transactional (ready to buy or convert). Matching your content to the dominant intent of a keyword is essential because Google ranks pages that satisfy intent, not pages that simply use the keyword.
Each page should target one primary keyword that defines its main topic, plus 5 to 20 closely related secondary keywords and long-tail variations that share the same search intent. Targeting more than one primary keyword per page leads to keyword cannibalization and confuses Google about what the page is really about.
Conduct a full keyword research project at least once a year and refresh individual topic clusters whenever you plan new content. Review your tracked rankings weekly or monthly so you spot opportunities and drops early. Search trends shift quickly, especially in fast-moving industries, so periodic re-research is essential.
